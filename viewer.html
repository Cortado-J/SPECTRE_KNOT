Where the grid points sit

Each grid point is where two strands cross. Moving the grid points changes the knot, but every version here is alternating: follow any strand and it goes over, under, over.

Per contact: the 8 tile neighbourhoods

Put one grid point wherever a spectre touches a neighbour, however many edges the contact spans. Black dots are corners where three or more tiles meet; the blue dots are the grid points. In this patch each spectre has between 4 and 7 contacts, 5.6 on average, so the plane has 2.8 grid points per spectre. The rarest neighbourhoods (under 2%) are left out.

Denser grids: fanning each tile

No arrangement of unit squares, triangles, hexagons and rhombi fits exactly inside the spectre with edges meeting whole: every attempt jams at one of its four right-angled corners. Instead, every point in a small region near the middle of the spectre can see its whole outline, so spokes from there cut it into sectors without leaving the tile. Each spoke adds one grid point; the open dots are the new ones.

Why not simply leave some of the 14 edges out of a single tile design? Edges of neighbouring spectres pair up in 31 different ways in this patch, and following those pairings links all 14 edges into one family. A design drawn the same way on every tile therefore has to treat every edge the same. The per-contact grid gets round this by looking at neighbours rather than at edges. It is also what you get from the every-edge grid by setting all but one grid point along each shared run to a break that turns back inside its own tile.

One thread through the whole plane

Can a single infinite thread pass through every spectre and still weave over, under, over? Yes. Here is the construction, why it gives exactly one thread, and why that thread can never repeat. One thing to be clear about first: this thread is aperiodic because it fills the plane, not because of the spectre. A spiral on graph paper would be non-repeating for the same reason. The tiling here is only scaffolding. The section after this one asks the harder question, whether a design that is the same on every tile, and so aperiodic only because the tiling is, can be a single thread.

The construction

At each grid point choose either a crossing or a break, where both strands turn back inside their own tile. Picture each tile as a disc. A crossing is exactly what a half-twisted band looks like when drawn flat: its two edges swap sides once. A break is no band at all. So the knot is the edge of the surface made from the tile discs plus one twisted band for every crossing.

Now pick a spanning tree of the tiles: a set of contacts that links every tile to every other along exactly one route. Make those contacts crossings and every other grid point a break.

Why it is one thread

Finite patch. Discs glued along a tree make a single disc. No band ever closes a loop, and each half twist can be undone by flipping a disc over, so there's nothing to change the shape. A disc has one edge, so the knot is one closed thread. The viewer offers two trees. Random tree is a uniform random spanning tree (Wilson's algorithm). Spiral orders the tiles along an outward spiral and keeps the links that run along it, adding links between neighbouring turns only where needed to join everything up. The 7,839-tile patch gets 7,837 crossings on its tree and a break at every other grid point, and the engine counts exactly one thread inside the disc.

Whole plane. Use an infinite spanning tree with one end: any two routes out to infinity eventually merge. The spiral makes this easy to picture: the thread winds outward turn after turn, and the only way to infinity is further round the spiral. More generally such a tree always exists. The tile neighbour graph of any tiling of the plane by bounded tiles is one-ended, and every countable connected graph has a normal spanning tree, which has the same ends as the graph (Diestel, Graph Theory, §8.2). Discs glued along a one-ended tree make a surface shaped like a closed half-plane, whose edge is a single line. So the knot is one thread, infinite in both directions, passing every tile and every grid point. A tree with two ends would give two threads, which is why the one-end condition matters.

Why it still alternates

Colour the regions between strands in two tones, as the Fill option does. A break only merges two regions of the same tone, so the rule that fixes over and under at each crossing stays consistent. Along the thread, over and under keep alternating. The viewer checks this in every patch it builds and finds no exceptions.

Why it can never repeat

Suppose a translation carried the thread onto itself. It cannot reverse the thread's direction, because that would leave a point fixed. So it slides the thread along itself, and the whole thread is made of copies of one finite stretch laid end to end. That keeps it within a fixed distance of a single straight line. But the thread visits every tile, including tiles as far from that line as you like. So no single thread that fills the plane can be periodic, on any grid. On the spectre the grid itself never repeats either, so nothing in the design lines up with a shifted copy of itself.

More crossings

The tree gives one crossing per tile, which is about a third of the grid points at 2.8 per spectre. More are possible. At any break, exactly one of the other two settings keeps a single thread: the crossing or the break that hugs the corner instead. Which one depends on the direction the thread takes on its two passes, so crossings can be added one at a time, checking as you go.

One tile as the unit cell

In a repeating pattern you design one cell and the lattice copies it. Can the spectre work the same way: set each grid point once, on one tile, and let the tiling set them all?

Grid points on the edges give you three designs, one of them a knot

A grid point on a tile edge is shared with the neighbour across that edge, so a one-tile design has to give the same state to both edges that meet there. In this tiling edge 1 meets edges 4, 6, 8, 10 or 12 of the neighbour; edge 2 meets 3, 5, 7, 9 or 11; and so on. Each edge meets three to five others, always an odd-numbered edge against an even-numbered one, and following these pairings links all fourteen edges into one chain. So every edge point must get the same state. That leaves three designs. All crossings is the 7-per-spectre knot. All turn-back makes every tile its own separate ring. All round-the-ends makes a ring round every vertex. Alternation is not the problem; the last two are perfectly alternating, they're just not knots. The problem is that there is only one real choice.

Two ways round it. Either let the state depend on which edges meet (31 different pairings occur, so 331 designs, all still set by a rule rather than tile by tile), or put the grid points inside the tile, where nothing is shared.

Grid points inside the tile give you 3G designs

Cut the tile into sectors with spokes from its centre point. Each spoke carries a grid point that belongs to this tile alone, so it can take any of the three states. With G spokes that is 3G designs, all with the same edge points: 27 for three spokes, 2,187 for seven, about 4.8 million for fourteen. Every one of them is alternating. The crossings form a planar network with four strands at each node, and any such network has a consistent over-under; the odd-against-even matching of the edges is what lets a single over-under rule on one tile agree with all its neighbours. The viewer checks alternation on every design it builds.

The families overlap and include a lot of debris. Setting every spoke to turn back leaves only the edge crossings; setting the edge points to turn back as well gives a bag of unlinked rings. The interesting designs are the mixed ones, and each has its own rosette in every tile. Pushing the edge crossings inside the tile too, so that only strands cross the boundary, gives the same family with each edge crossing doubled into a twist.

Can a one-tile design be a single thread?

This is the question the spanning-tree thread dodges. A design that is the same on every tile is non-repeating only because the tiling is, so a single thread of that kind would be aperiodic in the strong sense. On a repeating tiling the answer is no, by the sliding argument above. On the spectre the answer is yes. Design 0.1:01 is one infinite thread with no closed loops. On the 14-spoke fan it is 01222222222222: a crossing on spoke 0, a turn-back on spoke 1, and round-the-ends on every other spoke, which is the same as having no spoke there at all. That is nine grid points per tile: the seven shared edge points and two inside, where the ribbons cross once and turn back once. The proof is an induction up the supertile levels. Its step is a finite computation that is the same at every level, which is what makes it a proof rather than a search; the one input from outside is the spectre paper’s theorem that every level of the hierarchy is built by the same combinatorial rule.

A parity test that rules designs out

Two-colour the regions between strands, as the Fill option does. Take any finite set X of cell-coloured faces whose boundary crossings B satisfy one extra condition: every cell-coloured face touches an even number of them (X is a finite bicycle of the checkerboard graph). Then the diagram has at least two strands. Follow a strand: at each point it runs between one face of each colour, and a crossing swaps both neighbours. The black neighbour flips in or out of X exactly at B-crossings, and so does the white neighbour's membership of the region Y enclosed by B, so the parity [black ∈ X] + [white ∈ Y] never changes along a strand and is 0 far away. A single strand would force that parity to be 0 on every edge of the diagram, but at a B-crossing the white face between the inside and outside black faces would then have to be both in Y and out of it. The plain 7-point weave fails this test with X a single tile. The all-crossings 7-spoke design fails it with a 48-face X. Counting grid points inside the tile instead: every design with none or one grows closed loops within a level-1 supertile. With two there are 364 designs. Of these, 34 have no closed loop in any supertile up to level 7, and they come in identical pairs, so there are 17 different knots. The rest of this section proves 0.1:01, the one whose two crossing strands are shortest. (The 7-spoke design 0222221 is another of the 17, written 0.11:01, and was proved earlier by the same method.)

What a supertile looks like from outside

The strands inside a level-n supertile depend only on its type, so all that matters to the next level is how they pair up the ports on its boundary: two per unit edge, one near each end. Composing the children's pairings gives the parent's exactly, with no rim effects, and the cost grows only with the boundary (level 9 has 4.4 million rim edges and 133 million tiles). For design 0.1:01 the pairing of every supertile, of all nine types, at every level from 1 to 9, has the same shape. At every rim vertex but two, the strand that arrives on one edge turns straight back out along the other: a hook. At the two exceptions, which are always a pair of rim vertices of the supertile's mystic (Γ) child, the four ports are joined as a crossover. No strand closes up inside. The tile itself is the same: twelve hooks and one crossover, at its vertices 0 and 1. Those are the two ends of edge 0. The two strands that meet edge 0 cross once inside the tile and leave through edges 13 and 1. A supertile's crossover is the same thing: it is one particular tile's own crossover, passed up from Γ child to Γ child, so its two corners are always the two ends of one rim edge.

Why only parities matter

Glue two such children along a shared run of n unit edges. Every interior vertex of the run carries a hook in both children, and a strand crosses an edge diagonally, from the port near one end on one side to the port near the other end on the other side. So a strand entering the run from child c at end A hooks in c′ at the first vertex, crosses back, hooks in c at the second, and zigzags the whole way along, taking one edge per step and switching child at every one. It leaves at end B in c if n is even and in c′ if n is odd, and the strand coming the other way does the opposite. A run is therefore a four-port device whose behaviour depends on n only through n mod 2, and the two strands through it collect the run's hooks alternately. Everything else happens at the marks: the vertices where a child's neighbour changes, and the crossover vertices. The parent's pairing is a walk that steps from mark to mark through the children's hooks and crossovers and the runs' parities. For a Δ supertile that is 8 children, 24 marks, 52 runs and a walk on 104 ports.

The induction

Call a level-n supertile regular if its pairing is hooks everywhere except one crossover. The claim is that every supertile of every type and level is regular, with no loop inside, and with the crossover at the Γ child's corner. Level 1 is checked directly. For the step, take the children regular. By the zigzag argument their composition is fixed by the reduced data: which children share which runs, each run's parity, where the marks are. Extracting that data from the exact geometry at every level from 2 to 9 gives the same answer at levels 2, 4, 6, 8 and the same (mirror-image) answer at 3, 5, 7, 9; the handedness alternates because the substitution reflects once per level. Running the reduced walk on those two data sets gives a regular parent with the crossover at the Γ corner, in both cases, and the result agrees with the exact composition at every level. So regular children make a regular parent, at every level.

Why the reduced data keeps its period-2 pattern beyond level 9. The spectre’s discoverers prove that every tile lies in a unique hierarchy (Theorem 2.2), and that, through hats and turtles (Theorems 3.1, 4.1), the supertiles at each level are combinatorially mirror images of the same nine marked hexagons (Theorem 5.1). So the arrangement of children, and which side of which child meets which side of which neighbour, is the same at every level; only lengths change. Each side of a level-(n+1) supertile is a fixed sum of sides of its children, so the side lengths obey one linear recurrence (growing by φ³ ≈ 4.236 per level), and so do their parities, mod 2. The reduced data at level n+1 is read off the parities at level n and the fixed pattern, and records all of them, so it evolves deterministically on a finite set of states. It repeats from level 2 to level 4 and from 3 to 5, and therefore at every level. The same holds for the nest inputs below. (D. Smith, J. S. Myers, C. S. Kaplan, C. Goodman-Strauss, A chiral aperiodic monotile, Combinatorial Theory 4(2), 2024; arXiv:2305.17743.)

Merging

Regularity says what a supertile looks like from outside; it does not say its arcs are one strand. For that, follow a nest. Nb is the level-b Δ at the fixed point, Nb+1 the Δ that has it as child 1, and so on. Give each of the arcs of Nb its own label and carry the labels up through the same reduced walks: the hooks along a run split into two classes by position, the hooks at marks and the two crossover arcs are tracked individually, and the stretch of Nb's rim that lies on Nb+1's rim (8, 34, 144, 610, 2584 edges, every third Fibonacci number) is carried as a block until it is swallowed at the next level. The outcome is the same for b and b+2, because every input to the walk is: the reduced data, and where the labelled vertices fall in the next level's runs, modulo 2.

Putting it together

Nb lies strictly inside Nb+2, and the base block's distance to the rim of Nk grows by about 2.8 per level (3.6, 8.6, 23, 67, 184, 516, 1442, 4046 edge lengths at k = 2 … 9), so the nest covers the plane: this is the spectre tiling we are drawing. Any two tiles lie in some Nb; every arc of either tile is part of an arc of Nb, because no strand closes inside; and all arcs of Nb lie on one arc of Nb+4. So the two tiles are on the same strand. A closed loop would lie inside some Nb and would have been counted there. One infinite thread, through every tile, aperiodic in the strong sense.

Two remarks. The other 16 two-point designs can be put through the same machine, as 0222221 was; the all-crossings design and the plain weave fail before it starts, by growing closed loops (149,792 of them by level 7 for the weave). And the result is about this tiling. A spectre tiling with an infinite fault, two infinite-order supertiles meeting along a line, is not covered by the nest argument.

In the viewer the strand still looks cut into pieces: the patch is a disc, and the one strand leaves and re-enters it many times. The pieces are chords of the same strand.
