Why it is one strand

Seven steps. Read them as a few easy sentences, or open any step for an explanation, the details, or the rigorous version. Choose a depth for the whole page below, or go deeper one step at a time. The full argument is in the write-up of the proof (PDF), with its programs (zip).

How deep?

Status: an AI-written proof, not checked by a mathematician

The argument below was developed and written by an AI system. I haven’t verified all of it, and no mathematician has reviewed it. So it is a claim, not an established result. How this was made.

What is claimed

On the spectre tiling drawn on this site, the ribbon pieces of all the tiles join into one endless strand, with no closed loops.

Each step is marked by what supports it: cited from the spectre paper, computed by machine, or argued in words. Step 7 says exactly which tilings are covered.

Status. Everything finite is computed exactly, up to 133 million tiles. The step that carries it to every size, that a small finite map repeats with period two for ever, is now shown: it follows from the spectre paper’s Theorems 4.1 and 5.1 (every level is assembled by the same combinatorial pattern) together with exact computation (step 5). The last link, that the strands of every block of the nest merge, follows from the same pattern and is checked by machine for every block, not only the six computed outright (step 6). What the whole argument still takes on citation is the spectre paper’s description of how supertiles are assembled; that, and what is not covered, is in step 7.

Map of the proof: what rests on what

Each box is one fact; an arrow means ‘is used to prove’. The border says what supports it: computed by machine, cited from the spectre paper, argued in words. Click a box to open that step.

1

Tiles come in families, for ever

cited

Spectre tiles group into clusters called supertiles, those group into bigger supertiles, and so on without end, every level built by the same rule.

An explanation

In any spectre tiling the tiles come in clusters of eight or nine. There are nine kinds of cluster, named by Greek letters (Γ, Δ, Θ, Λ, Ξ, Π, Σ, Φ, Ψ). The clusters themselves come in clusters of eight or nine, of the same nine kinds, and so on up. Every tile belongs to a level-1 supertile, which belongs to a level-2 supertile, and so on for ever.

This family tree is why the tiling can never repeat, and it is the ladder the whole proof climbs. Going up one level multiplies the number of tiles by about 8, and the length of the outline (the rim) by about 4.2.

In detail

The block sizes of the Δ supertiles are 1, 9, 71, 559, 4,401, 34,649, … tiles, with rims of 14, 46, 182, 758, 3,198, 13,534 tile edges. The tiling drawn on this site is built from a nest: N1 ⊂ N2 ⊂ N3 ⊂ …, where each block is child number 1 of the next. Every picture on the site is a piece of it.

That every tile has such a hierarchy, and that it is unique, is Theorem 2.2 of the spectre paper. That every level is built by the same combinatorial rule comes from its Theorems 3.1, 4.1 and 5.1 (step 5 says how that is used).

2

From outside, a supertile is a black box

argued

All the outside world can tell about a supertile is which points on its outline its ribbons join. So we can work out a big supertile from its pieces without looking inside them.

An explanation

Where two tiles meet along an edge, the ribbon crosses the edge at one point and crosses it on a slant: it arrives near one end of the edge and leaves near the other. So on each side of an edge there are two places a ribbon can be, one near each end. Call them ports. A tile has 14 edges, so 28 ports, and its drawing joins them in pairs.

The same is true of a supertile: each ribbon inside runs from one rim port to another (or closes into a ring, if rings exist). That pairing is everything the outside sees. So a supertile’s pairing can be found from its children’s pairings alone: put the children side by side, start at a rim port, follow the ribbon from port to port through the children until it comes out on the rim again.

In detail

The work grows with the length of the rim, not with the number of tiles. That is why a computer can do it exactly, for every supertile of all nine kinds, up to level 9, where a supertile has about 133 million tiles. The program never looks inside a child: it follows ports. A ring would show up as ports left over that never reach the rim.

The pairing computed this way was checked against the drawn ribbons for every supertile of levels 1 to 3, all nine kinds: they agree port for port. The pictures on this site are drawn by the separate drawing program.

3

Every supertile has the same simple signature

computed

Seen from outside, every supertile, of every kind and size, looks the same: the ribbon hooks round every corner of its outline except two, where two ribbons cross. And there is no closed loop inside.

An explanation

At every corner of the rim except two, the ribbon that arrives on one edge turns straight round and leaves on the next edge: a hook. At the two remaining corners, the two ends of one edge, the ribbons swap partners and cross: the crossover. The single tile already looks like this: 12 hooks and one crossover. So does every supertile found by the computer.

The same holds for the 71-tile block: 182 edges, 180 corner U-turns, one crossover.

In detail

The computer found this signature for every supertile of every kind at every level from 1 to 9. The crossover always sits at the same place: the corner of the supertile’s Γ child, and it is one particular tile’s own crossover, handed up from Γ child to Γ child. No ring appears inside at any level.

The pictures above are a separate check of levels 0, 1 and 2: they are computed on this page’s build from the drawn ribbons, not from the port program, and the build stops if the count ever differs from “hooks at every corner but two”.

On its own, “true up to level 9” says nothing about level 10. Steps 4 and 5 turn it into every level.

4

Along a shared run, only even or odd matters

argued

Where two such supertiles meet along a run of edges, a ribbon zigzags along the run and comes out at the far end, on the same side if the run is even, on the other side if it is odd. Nothing else about the run matters.

An explanation

Put two supertiles side by side so that they share a run of edges, with hooks at every corner along it. Follow a ribbon into the run: it hooks round the first corner in one supertile, crosses the next edge on a slant into the other, hooks round the next corner there, crosses back, and so on. It moves forward one edge at every step and changes side at every step. So after the last edge it is on the side it came in on if the run has an even number of edges, and on the other side if odd.

This is the step that makes the problem finite. A rim has thousands of corners, but along a shared run all that detail boils down to one bit: even or odd.

In detail

Two ribbons use the run: one entering at each end, between them taking every hook on the run, alternately. The zigzag needs every corner along the run to be a hook on both sides. Corners along a run are, by definition, not marks (a mark is a corner where a child’s neighbour changes, or a crossover corner), and every corner that is not a hook is a crossover corner, which is always a mark. It also needs the slanted crossing at every edge, which is the one rule used on every edge of every tile. Because the zigzag always moves forward, no ring can hide inside a run.

5

A small map, the same at every level: dominoes

computedcited: Thms 4.1, 5.1

So building a supertile from its children is a small, finite puzzle, and the puzzle repeats from level to level. If one level has the signature, the next does too: the signature passes up like falling dominoes.

An explanation

To work out a supertile’s pairing from its children we only need a small map: the children, the runs along which they touch, whether each run is even or odd, and the marks (the corners where a child’s neighbour changes, and the children’s crossovers). Following a ribbon becomes a walk from mark to mark: a child’s hook or crossover, then a run’s even-or-odd rule, then the next child, until it reaches the rim.

The domino argument (induction): if every level-n supertile has the signature, the walk on the small map gives every level-(n+1) supertile the signature too, with no ring. Levels 1 and 2 were computed outright, so every domino falls.

In detail

For a Δ supertile the small map has 8 children, 52 runs and 24 marks, whatever the level, and a walk visits at most 104 ports. The small map, read off the real geometry, is exactly the same at levels 2, 4, 6 and 8, and exactly the same at levels 3, 5, 7 and 9, for all nine kinds. Odd and even levels give two different maps; the only thing that changes between them is on which side of the crossover corner its partner corner lies.

Why it repeats for ever. From the spectre paper: every tile lies in a unique endless hierarchy (Theorem 2.2); Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 turn a spectre tiling into a tiling by nine marked hexagons; Theorem 5.1 says those group into supertiles that are, combinatorially, reflected versions of the same nine hexagons. So every level is assembled by the same pattern (checked against the computed hierarchy at every level from 1 to 8). Each supertile has six sides, and each side of a bigger supertile is a row of whole sides of its children, so the side lengths obey one fixed rule: multiply by a fixed 54-by-54 matrix. Their parities (even or odd) turn out never to change at all. The small map is a function of those parities and of which side of the crossover corner its partner lies on, which simply alternates. So the small map alternates for ever. This was rebuilt from parities alone and matches the real small map, for all nine kinds, at every level from 2 to 9.

6

Grow a nest, and everything joins

computedcited: Thms 4.1, 5.1

Take a block, the block around it, the block around that, and so on. Each time, the pieces of the small block lie on fewer strands, until they lie on one. The blocks grow to cover the whole plane, so any two tiles end up on the same strand.

An explanation

The signature says how a supertile looks from outside. It does not yet say that the pieces inside belong to one ribbon. For that, label every ribbon piece of a small block and carry the labels up the nest using the same mark-to-mark walks. Each time we go up a level, labels on different ribbons find themselves on the same one. Four levels up, every label is on a single ribbon.

The big numbers one level up are pieces lying along the part of the block’s edge that is also the rim of the next block: they are gathered at the level after.

In detail

This was computed two ways, exactly (following every ribbon one by one, for the first six blocks of the nest) and with the small map; they agree. Then for every block: every input the small-map version reads is fixed by the assembly pattern, the parities and which way the crossover points. A separate program rebuilds those inputs from that data alone, and they match the real ones for blocks 1 to 6 at every level up to 9. A third program follows the side lengths as exact formulas instead of numbers, and checks every comparison of lengths the computation makes, 2,106 in all; each holds for the real lengths at every level. So block b and block b+2 get identical inputs, for every b. The result: for an even-numbered block, its pieces lie on one strand three levels up; for an odd-numbered one, four.

The nest covers the plane. Each block shares no corner with the rim of its grandparent: in the fixed assembly pattern, the only side of the small block on its parent’s rim lies inside a side of the parent that faces a sibling one level up. So every tile next to block Nb is inside Nb+2, and the blocks swallow every tile eventually. (Also measured directly for the first five blocks.)

The finish. Take any two tiles. Both lie inside some block Nb of the nest. Every ribbon piece in them is part of a piece of Nb, because nothing closes up inside, and all pieces of Nb lie on one ribbon of Nb+4. So the two tiles are on the same ribbon. And there is no ring: a ring is finite, so the smallest supertile containing it would have found it when assembled from its children, and the count is zero at every assembly.

7

Exactly what is covered

The one-strand argument is for the tiling drawn on this site, the one grown from a nest, and for no other. Other spectre tilings whose supertiles fill the plane have no closed loops either, but one strand is not guaranteed: computer checks suggest that some have three. Tilings with ‘fault lines’ are not covered.

An explanation

A spectre tiling is determined by its chain of ancestors. The tiling here grows by always taking child number 1 (the nest). Other tilings grow from a different chain of ancestors. For all of them whose supertiles fill the plane, the “no closed loops” part of the argument applies unchanged. The “one strand” part depends on the chain, and it appears not to hold for every chain: along one chain, with Θ and Γ supertiles alternating (child 2, then child 7), the computer finds three strands that do not join, followed exactly to level 8 and by the label method to level 20. That is evidence, not a proof, and the site claims nothing about it. A few special tilings contain two or more infinite supertiles meeting along lines or at a point (fault tilings); those are not covered at all.

In detail

The design is the same on every tile in the strict sense: the nest tiling uses only one handedness of the spectre, so it is one drawing, turned and slid, never flipped. Over and under is a separate question: any drawing of this kind can be given a weave that alternates along every ribbon. The single-strand result is about which pieces join.

Glossary
Port

The one point on each tile edge where ribbons cross it, 0.15 of the edge off its middle.

Supertile

A cluster of tiles (9, 71, 559, …) that behaves like one big tile. There are nine kinds, named by Greek letters, and every level is built from the one below by the same rule.

Level

Level 0 is a single tile, level 1 a supertile of tiles, level 2 a supertile of level-1 supertiles, and so on.

Rim

The outline of a supertile. Only the ports on the rim matter from outside.

Hook

At a corner of the rim, a ribbon that comes in through the port just before the corner and goes straight back out through the port just after it.

Crossover

At one special corner (the Γ corner) the two ribbons do not hook back; they cross and leave by the far side.

Signature

What a supertile looks like from outside: a hook at every corner but two, one crossover, and no ring inside.

Run

A stretch of edges along which two neighbouring supertiles touch.

Even, odd

Whether a run (or a side) has an even or odd number of tile edges. The zigzag along a run only cares about this.

Mark

A point on a child’s rim where something happens: its neighbour changes, or its crossover sits.

Small map

The children, their runs (even or odd) and their marks: all you need to work out how ribbons join inside a supertile.

Nest

A chain of blocks, each one child of the next, growing to fill the whole plane. The tiling on this site is grown this way.
