Why it is one strand

Proof of the following two theorems:

The Endless Strand Theorem

In every spectre tiling whose supertiles fill the plane, with the Spectre Knot design on each tile, no strand ever closes into a loop.

Every strand runs on for ever in both directions. Steps 1–5; scope in step 7.

The Ariadne Theorem

In the nest tiling drawn on this site, with the Spectre Knot design on each tile, the ribbon pieces of all the tiles join into one endless strand.

The nest tiling is grown outwards from a single tile, each block sitting in the same place (child number 1) inside the next. Step 6.

The proofs, and their Lean version, were written with AI: how this was made, and what has been checked.

1

Tiles come in families, for ever

citedargued

Spectre tiles group into clusters called supertiles, those group into bigger supertiles, and so on without end, every level built by the same rule.

An explanation

In any spectre tiling the tiles come in clusters of eight or nine. There are nine kinds of cluster, named by Greek letters (Γ, Δ, Θ, Λ, Ξ, Π, Σ, Φ, Ψ). The clusters themselves group into bigger clusters of eight (seven for Γ), of the same nine kinds, and so on up. Every tile belongs to a level-1 supertile, which belongs to a level-2 supertile, and so on for ever.

This family tree is why the tiling can never repeat, and it is the ladder the whole proof climbs. Going up one level multiplies the number of tiles by about 8, and the length of the outline (the rim) by about 4.2.

In detail

The block sizes of the Δ supertiles are 1, 9, 71, 559, 4,401, 34,649, … tiles, with rims of 14, 46, 182, 758, 3,198, 13,534 tile edges. The tiling drawn on this site is built from a nest: N1 ⊂ N2 ⊂ N3 ⊂ …, where each block is child number 1 of the next. Every picture on the site is a piece of it.

That every tile has such a hierarchy, and that it is unique, comes from the spectre paper’s Theorems 2.2, 3.1 and 4.1 (Theorem 4.1 groups the tiles into the first supertiles; the uniqueness is part of Theorem 2.2). That every level is built by the same combinatorial rule comes from its Theorems 3.1, 4.1 and 5.1 (step 5 says how that is used). The write-up restates both as its Theorem 2.1.

Two supertiles of the same kind are copies of each other, turned and slid, down to the number of every tile inside: the write-up’s Lemma 2.6 (rigidity).

2

From outside, a supertile is a black box

arguedLean

All the outside world can tell about a supertile is which points on its outline its ribbons join. So we can work out a big supertile from its pieces without looking inside them.

An explanation

Where two tiles meet along an edge, the ribbon crosses the edge at one point and crosses it on a slant: it arrives near one end of the edge and leaves near the other. So on each side of an edge there are two places a ribbon can be, one near each end. Call them ports. A tile has 14 edges, so 28 ports, and its drawing joins them in pairs.

The same is true of a supertile: each ribbon inside runs from one rim port to another (or closes into a ring, if rings exist). That pairing is everything the outside sees. So a supertile’s pairing can be found from its children’s pairings alone: put the children side by side, start at a rim port, follow the ribbon from port to port through the children until it comes out on the rim again.

In detail

The work grows with the length of the rim, not with the number of tiles. That is why a computer can do it for every supertile of all nine kinds up to level 9, where a supertile has about 133 million tiles (in floating point, as a check; the proof itself needs only level 1, done in exact arithmetic). The program never looks inside a child: it follows ports. A ring would show up as ports left over that never reach the rim.

The pairing computed this way was checked against the drawn ribbons for every supertile of levels 1 to 3, all nine kinds: they agree port for port. The pictures on this site are drawn by the separate drawing program.

In the write-up, the drawing on the tile is Definition 3.1, the rule for carrying a ribbon across an edge Definition 3.2, and the strands are the connected pieces of the strand graph, Definition 3.3. That a patch of tiles acts as a black box (a pairing of its rim ports, plus any rings inside) is Lemma 4.1, and that the box of a glued patch comes from the boxes of its parts and their shared edges alone is Lemma 4.2.

3

Every supertile has the same simple signature

computedarguedLean

Seen from outside, every supertile, of every kind and size, looks the same: the ribbon hooks round every corner of its outline except two, where two ribbons cross. And there is no closed loop inside.

An explanation

At every corner of the rim except two, the ribbon that arrives on one edge turns straight round and leaves on the next edge: a hook. At the two remaining corners, the two ends of one edge, the ribbons swap partners and cross: the crossover. The single tile already looks like this: 12 hooks and one crossover. So does every supertile found by the computer.

The same holds for the 71-tile block: 182 edges, 180 corner U-turns, one crossover.

In detail

The signature is the write-up’s Definition 4.3. A single tile has it, and so does the Mystic, the pair of tiles that every level-1 supertile holds as its Γ child (Lemma 4.4). The computer found it for every supertile of every kind at every level from 1 to 9. The crossover always sits at the same place: the corner of the supertile’s Γ child, and it is one particular tile’s own crossover, handed up from Γ child to Γ child. No ring appears inside at any level.

The pictures above are a separate check of levels 0, 1 and 2: they are computed on this page’s build from the drawn ribbons, not from the port program, and the build stops if the count ever differs from “hooks at every corner but two”.

On its own, “true up to level 9” says nothing about level 10. Steps 4 and 5 turn a check at level 1 into every level.

4

Along a shared run, only even or odd matters

arguedLean

Where two such supertiles meet along a run of edges, a ribbon zigzags along the run and comes out at the far end, on the same side if the run is even, on the other side if it is odd. Nothing else about the run matters.

An explanation

Put two supertiles side by side so that they share a run of edges, with hooks at every corner along it. Follow a ribbon into the run: it hooks round the first corner in one supertile, crosses the next edge on a slant into the other, hooks round the next corner there, crosses back, and so on. It moves forward one edge at every step and changes side at every step. So after the last edge it is on the side it came in on if the run has an even number of edges, and on the other side if odd.

This is the step that makes the problem finite. A rim has thousands of corners, but along a shared run all that detail boils down to one bit: even or odd. In particular, a run two edges longer behaves exactly like the shorter one.

In detail

This is the Zigzag Lemma, the write-up’s Lemma 5.1. Two ribbons use the run: one entering at each end, between them taking every hook on the run, alternately. The zigzag needs every corner along the run to be a hook on both sides. Corners along a run are, by definition, not marks (a mark is a corner where a child’s neighbour changes, or a crossover corner), and every corner that is not a hook is a crossover corner, which is always a mark. It also needs the slanted crossing at every edge, which is the one rule used on every edge of every tile. Because the zigzag always moves forward, no ring can hide inside a run.

Gluing all the children of a supertile this way gives its box from theirs: every corner of its outline that is not a mark is a hook, and every ribbon can be followed to the rim run by run (Proposition 5.4); Corollary 5.5 says where each child’s hook ends up.

5

Two more edges change nothing: from level 1 to every level

computedarguedcited: Thms 2.2, 3.1, 4.1, 5.1Lean

Make a shared run two edges longer and the ribbons come out exactly where they did before: they just zigzag round two more corners. A big supertile is built like a small one, only with every side longer by an even number of edges. So checking the smallest supertiles once settles every size: the signature passes up like falling dominoes.

An explanation

To build a supertile from its children (step 2), put the children side by side and follow ribbons from port to port. With the children’s sides as long as at level 1, the computer did this for all nine kinds, each with the crossover pointing either way: 18 assemblies. Every one comes out with the signature, and no ring.

At a higher level the children are bigger, so every side is longer. But it is longer by an even number of edges, at every level: the side lengths grow, but even stays even and odd stays odd. And making a side two edges longer is harmless. Where two children share that side, the ribbons zigzag across the two extra corners and come out where they did before (step 4). Where the side is on the outline, the two extra corners are just two more hooks. So the assembly at any level can be reached from the level-1 one, two edges at a time, without changing anything that matters.

That is the domino argument: if every level-n supertile has the signature, so does every level-(n+1) supertile, because its assembly is the level-1 assembly with longer sides. Level 1 was computed outright, so every domino falls.

In detail

Each of the 18 level-1 assemblies has 8 children (7 for Γ), 732 ports (640) and an outline of 182 edges (160). They were computed twice: by a program that builds them from the assembly tables and the level-1 side lengths alone, and again by the Lean proof assistant, a program that checks proofs step by step. All 18 have the signature (Computation 7.3), and so do the nine level-1 supertiles themselves, the base case (Computation 7.5).

Each side’s length depends only on its letter (Proposition 6.1), and every side length is a Fibonacci number, twice one, or one plus or minus two (Proposition 6.2), so the lengths only grow and their evenness never changes (Corollary 6.3). One side needs care: in the Σ child the crossover sits in the middle of a side (Lemma 6.4), so extra edges are never added right next to it, and edges added before it move it along by two; every side is at least five edges long (Lemma 6.5). With that, the lengths of any level are reached from level 1 two edges at a time (Lemma 7.2, reachability), and two extra edges change nothing (Lemma 7.1, lengthening). So the assemblies have the signature at every level (Proposition 7.4), and with the base case every supertile of every level has it: the Signature Theorem, Theorem 7.6.

Why every level is assembled the same way. From the spectre paper, restated as the write-up’s Theorem 2.1 (step 1): every tile lies in a unique endless hierarchy (Theorem 2.2); Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 turn a spectre tiling into a tiling by nine marked hexagons; Theorem 5.1 says those group into supertiles that are, combinatorially, reflected versions of the same nine hexagons. So every level is assembled by the same pattern, and each side of a bigger supertile is a row of whole sides of its children: the write-up’s Proposition 2.4, checked against the computed hierarchy at every level from 1 to 8.

Second checks. The same assemblies were also computed directly at the side lengths of levels 2 to 4, and with every single two-edge lengthening of the level-1 ones (1,792 of them, each with the crossover either way): all have the signature. An earlier, longer route (comparing reduced ‘skeletons’ of the assemblies level by level) gives the same result; it is kept only as a check.

6

Grow a nest, and everything joins: the Ariadne Theorem

computedarguedcited: Thms 2.2, 3.1, 4.1, 5.1Lean

Take a block, the block around it, the block around that, and so on. Each time, the pieces of the small block lie on fewer strands, until they lie on one. The blocks grow to cover the whole plane, so any two tiles end up on the same strand.

An explanation

The signature says how a supertile looks from outside. It does not yet say that the pieces inside belong to one ribbon. For that, look one level up at a time. When a block sits inside the next block of the nest, each of its ribbons, followed outwards, ends on one of just five hooks of the bigger block, at the corners where the bigger block’s outline passes from one child to the next; and which of the five depends, for almost all of the small block’s outline, only on which side the ribbon leaves from (a short table covers the corners and the two crossover ribbons). (Ribbons on the one side of the small block that lies on the bigger block’s outline stay where they are, as hooks of the bigger block.)

This rule was computed once, at the level-1 sizes, and the argument of step 5 carries it to every level: two extra edges change nothing. Then follow it up. The five hooks lie well inside the sides of the bigger block, so one level further up the rule applies to them again, side by side. Three levels up (for an even-numbered block) or four (odd), every ribbon of the small block has arrived on the same hook.

The big numbers one level up are pieces lying along the side of the block that is also part of the outline of the next block: they are gathered at the level after.

In detail

Number the stretches of the bigger block’s outline (each a side of one of its children) 0 to 21. The five hooks are at the corners where stretches 9, 12, 16, 17 and 21 start; call them junctions. Junctions 16 and 17 are the two ends of the small block’s side 2, the side on the outline. For an even-numbered small block, ribbons leaving its sides 0 and 1 go to junction 17, side 3 to 16, side 4 to 12, side 5 to 9; for an odd-numbered one, side 0 to 21, side 1 to 17, sides 3, 4 and 5 to 16. Junction 9 lies on side 2 of the bigger block, 12 on side 3, 16 and 17 on side 4, 21 on side 5. In the write-up this rule is Computation 8.6 at level 1 and Lemma 8.7 at every level; where the junctions lie is Lemma 8.8.

So for an even-numbered block every ribbon is, one level up, on a hook inside side 2, 3 or 4; two levels up, inside side 4; three levels up, on junction 12. For an odd-numbered block: inside side 4 or 5; then 3 or 2; then 4; then junction 12, four levels up. This gives exactly the counts in the last columns of the table, for every block N1, N2, N3, … (Merging, Theorem 8.5). The rule was computed twice at level 1, by a program and by the Lean proof assistant, and checked directly at the sizes of levels 2 to 4.

It was also checked the long way: following every ribbon one by one, for the first six blocks of the nest, up to 133 million tiles; and by an earlier, longer method that carries labels up the nest and checks 2,106 comparisons of side lengths for every block from the second on (1,980 hold because every side is at least 5 edges long; the other 126 were certified exactly, twice; the first block was computed directly). Both agree.

The nest covers the plane. The nest itself is the write-up’s Construction 8.1, and its blocks are well defined (Proposition 8.2). Each block shares no corner with the rim of its grandparent: in the fixed assembly pattern, the only side of the small block on its parent’s rim lies inside a side of the parent that faces a sibling one level up. So every tile next to block Nb is inside Nb+2, and the blocks swallow every tile eventually (the Grandparent Lemma, Lemma 8.3, and Proposition 8.4).

The finish. Take any two tiles. Both lie inside some block Nb of the nest. Every ribbon piece in them is part of an arc of Nb (a stretch of strand inside the block), because nothing closes up inside, and all arcs of Nb lie on one arc of Nb+4. So the two tiles are on the same strand. And there is no ring: a ring is finite, so the smallest supertile containing it would contain a ring, and no supertile does (step 5). Every strand is either a ring or runs on for ever both ways (Lemma 3.4), so this one runs on for ever: the Ariadne Theorem, Theorem B.

7

Exactly what is covered

arguedLean

The one-strand argument is for the tiling drawn on this site, the one grown from a nest, and for no other. Other spectre tilings whose supertiles fill the plane have no closed loops either (every strand runs on for ever in both directions), but one strand is not guaranteed: computer checks suggest that some have three. Tilings with ‘fault lines’ are not covered.

An explanation

A spectre tiling is determined by its chain of ancestors. The tiling here grows by always taking child number 1 (the nest). Other tilings grow from a different chain of ancestors. For all of them whose supertiles fill the plane, the “no closed loops” part of the argument applies unchanged, so every strand runs on for ever in both directions; this part has also been checked in Lean, for any chain of supertiles. The “one strand” part depends on the chain, and it appears not to hold for every chain: along one chain, with Θ and Γ supertiles alternating (child 2, then child 7), the computer finds three strands that do not join, followed exactly to level 8 and by the label method to level 20. That is evidence, not a proof, and the site claims nothing about it. A few special tilings contain two or more infinite supertiles meeting along lines of tile edges (fault tilings); those are not covered at all.

In detail

The design is the same on every tile in the strict sense: the nest tiling uses only one handedness of the spectre, so it is one drawing, turned and slid, never flipped. Over and under is a separate question: any drawing of this kind can be given a weave that alternates along every ribbon. The single-strand result is about which pieces join.

The no-loop half is the Endless Strand Theorem, the write-up’s Theorem A: in every spectre tiling whose supertiles fill the plane, a closed strand would lie inside a single supertile, and none has one (step 5). The three strands along another chain are Remark 9.1: a computation, not a theorem.

Glossary
Port

The one point on each tile edge where ribbons cross it, 0.15 of the edge off its middle. (From step 2 on, as in the write-up of the proof, the word means the two ribbon ends of a tile at that point, one heading towards each end of the edge: 28 per tile.)

Supertile

A cluster of tiles (9, 71, 559, …) that behaves like one big tile. There are nine kinds, named by Greek letters, and every level is built from the one below by the same rule.

Level

Level 0 is a single tile, level 1 a supertile of tiles, level 2 a supertile of level-1 supertiles, and so on.

Rim

The outline of a supertile. Only the ports on the rim matter from outside.

Hook

At a corner of the rim, a ribbon that comes in through the port just before the corner and goes straight back out through the port just after it.

Crossover

At one special corner (the Γ corner) the two ribbons do not hook back; they cross and leave by the far side.

Signature

What a supertile looks like from outside: a hook at every corner but two, one crossover, and no ring inside.

Run

A stretch of edges along which two neighbouring supertiles touch.

Even, odd

Whether a run (or a side) has an even or odd number of tile edges. The zigzag along a run only cares about this.

Mark

A point on a child’s rim where something happens: its neighbour changes, or its crossover sits.

Move

Making one shared side two edges longer, on both children that share it. The ribbons zigzag round two more corners and come out where they did before, so a move changes nothing that matters (step 5).

Junction

A corner on a block’s outline where one child’s side ends and the next begins. Every ribbon of a block ends up, one level up, on the hook at one of five junctions (step 6).

Nest

A chain of blocks, each one child of the next, growing to fill the whole plane. The tiling on this site is grown this way.
