Two theorems, and why they hold

Eight steps: five of groundwork shared by both theorems, one for each theorem, and one on exactly what is covered. Read them as a few easy sentences, or open any step for an explanation, the details, or the rigorous version. Choose a depth for the whole page below, or go deeper one step at a time. The full argument is in the write-up of the proof (PDF), with its programs (zip).

How deep?

Status: an AI-written proof, not checked by a mathematician

The argument below was developed and written by an AI system. I haven’t verified all of it, and no mathematician has reviewed it. So it is a claim, not an established result. Its combinatorial core has also been checked by the Lean proof assistant, in a formal proof that the AI wrote too; the link to actual tilings, and the part of Claim 1 about tilings with fault lines, are not part of that check. How this was made.

Claim 1 · the Endless Strand Theorem

In every spectre tiling with the Spectre Knot design on each tile, no strand ever closes into a loop.

However the tiles cover the plane, every strand runs on for ever in both directions. Groundwork, then step 6.

Claim 2 · the Ariadne Theorem

In the nest tiling with the Spectre Knot design on each tile, all the ribbon pieces join into one endless strand.

The nest tiling, drawn on this site, grows outwards from a single tile, each block in the same place inside the next. Groundwork, then step 7.

Each step is marked by what supports it: cited from the spectre paper, computed by machine, or argued in words. Step 8 says exactly which tilings each theorem covers.

Status. The finite checks the proof needs are computed exactly: the single tile, the smallest supertiles (8 or 9 tiles), the 18 ways of assembling them into the next level, and, for the nest, where a block’s ribbons go one level up; larger cases, up to 133 million tiles, were computed too, as a cross-check. What carries these to every size is that two extra edges change nothing, and that every level’s sizes are those of the smallest supertiles made longer by even amounts (step 5); it rests on the spectre paper’s Theorems 4.1 and 5.1 (every level is assembled by the same combinatorial pattern). The same argument settles the merging of every block of the nest (step 7). What the whole argument takes on citation is the spectre paper’s description of how supertiles are assembled; that, and exactly which tilings each theorem covers, is in step 8.

Groundwork, shared by both theorems
1

Tiles come in families, for ever

cited

Spectre tiles group into clusters called supertiles, those group into bigger supertiles, and so on without end, every level built by the same rule.

An explanation

In any spectre tiling the tiles come in clusters of eight or nine. There are nine kinds of cluster, named by Greek letters (Γ, Δ, Θ, Λ, Ξ, Π, Σ, Φ, Ψ). The clusters themselves come in clusters of eight or nine, of the same nine kinds, and so on up. Every tile belongs to a level-1 supertile, which belongs to a level-2 supertile, and so on for ever.

This family tree is why the tiling can never repeat, and it is the ladder the whole proof climbs. Going up one level multiplies the number of tiles by about 8, and the length of the outline (the rim) by about 4.2.

In detail

The block sizes of the Δ supertiles are 1, 9, 71, 559, 4,401, 34,649, … tiles, with rims of 14, 46, 182, 758, 3,198, 13,534 tile edges. The tiling drawn on this site is the nest tiling: N1 ⊂ N2 ⊂ N3 ⊂ …, grown outwards from a single tile N0, where each block is child number 1 of the next. (Tiling theorists would call it a fixed point of the substitution: the tiling whose chain of child numbers is 1, 1, 1, ….) Every picture on the site is a piece of it.

That every tile has such a hierarchy, and that it is unique, is Theorem 2.2 of the spectre paper. That every level is built by the same combinatorial rule comes from its Theorems 3.1, 4.1 and 5.1 (step 5 says how that is used).

2

From outside, a supertile is a black box

argued

All the outside world can tell about a supertile is which points on its outline its ribbons join. So we can work out a big supertile from its pieces without looking inside them.

An explanation

Where two tiles meet along an edge, the ribbon crosses the edge at one point and crosses it on a slant: it arrives near one end of the edge and leaves near the other. So on each side of an edge there are two places a ribbon can be, one near each end. Call them ports. A tile has 14 edges, so 28 ports, and its drawing joins them in pairs.

The same is true of a supertile: each ribbon inside runs from one rim port to another (or closes into a ring, if rings exist). That pairing is everything the outside sees. So a supertile’s pairing can be found from its children’s pairings alone: put the children side by side, start at a rim port, follow the ribbon from port to port through the children until it comes out on the rim again.

In detail

The work grows with the length of the rim, not with the number of tiles. That is why a computer can do it for every supertile of all nine kinds up to level 9, where a supertile has about 133 million tiles. The program never looks inside a child: it follows ports. A ring would show up as ports left over that never reach the rim.

The pairing computed this way was checked against the drawn ribbons for every supertile of levels 1 to 3, all nine kinds: they agree port for port. The pictures on this site are drawn by the separate drawing program.

3

Every supertile has the same simple signature

computed

Seen from outside, every supertile, of every kind and size, looks the same: the ribbon hooks round every corner of its outline except two, where two ribbons cross. And there is no closed loop inside.

An explanation

At every corner of the rim except two, the ribbon that arrives on one edge turns straight round and leaves on the next edge: a hook. At the two remaining corners, the two ends of one edge, the ribbons swap partners and cross: the crossover. The single tile already looks like this: 12 hooks and one crossover. So does every supertile found by the computer.

The same holds for the 71-tile block: 182 edges, 180 corner U-turns, one crossover.

In detail

The computer found this signature for every supertile of every kind at every level from 1 to 9. The crossover always sits at the same place: the corner of the supertile’s Γ child, and it is one particular tile’s own crossover, handed up from Γ child to Γ child. No ring appears inside at any level.

The pictures above are a separate check of levels 0, 1 and 2: they are computed on this page’s build from the drawn ribbons, not from the port program, and the build stops if the count ever differs from “hooks at every corner but two”.

On its own, “true up to level 9” says nothing about level 10. The proof uses only levels 0 and 1, computed exactly; the higher levels are a cross-check. Steps 4 and 5 carry level 1 to every level.

4

Along a shared run, only even or odd matters

argued

Where two such supertiles meet along a run of edges, a ribbon zigzags along the run and comes out at the far end, on the same side if the run is even, on the other side if it is odd. Nothing else about the run matters.

An explanation

Put two supertiles side by side so that they share a run of edges, with hooks at every corner along it. Follow a ribbon into the run: it hooks round the first corner in one supertile, crosses the next edge on a slant into the other, hooks round the next corner there, crosses back, and so on. It moves forward one edge at every step and changes side at every step. So after the last edge it is on the side it came in on if the run has an even number of edges, and on the other side if odd.

This is the step that makes the problem finite. A rim has thousands of corners, but along a shared run all that detail boils down to one bit: even or odd. In particular, a run two edges longer behaves exactly like the shorter one.

In detail

Two ribbons use the run: one entering at each end, between them taking every hook on the run, alternately. The zigzag needs every corner along the run to be a hook on both sides. Corners along a run are, by definition, not marks (a mark is a corner where a child’s neighbour changes, or a crossover corner), and every corner that is not a hook is a crossover corner, which is always a mark. It also needs the slanted crossing at every edge, which is the one rule used on every edge of every tile. Because the zigzag always moves forward, no ring can hide inside a run.

5

Two more edges change nothing: from level 1 to every level

computedcited: Thms 4.1, 5.1

Make a shared run two edges longer and the ribbons come out exactly where they did before: they just zigzag round two more corners. A big supertile is built like a small one, only with every side longer by an even number of edges. So checking the smallest supertiles once settles every size: the signature passes up like falling dominoes.

An explanation

A supertile is assembled from its children, and once the children have the signature, all that matters is how their sides are glued: along each shared run of edges the ribbons zigzag, and only whether the run is even or odd decides where they come out (step 4). So lengthen a shared run by two edges, and nothing changes: the ribbons zigzag round two more corners and come out exactly where they did before, with no new loop.

Now the sizes. A side of a bigger supertile is a row of whole sides of its children, always the same row, so the side lengths at each level follow from those at the level below by one fixed rule. They turn out to be Fibonacci numbers, twice one, or one plus or minus two: they only grow, and each stays even or stays odd for ever. So a supertile at any level is assembled exactly like one at level 1, only with every side longer by an even number of edges, and it can be reached from the level-1 assembly two edges at a time.

The domino argument: the 18 level-1 assemblies (nine kinds, with the crossover either way) were computed outright, every ribbon followed, and all have the signature. Lengthening keeps it. So every assembly at every level has it, and if every level-n supertile has the signature, so does every level-(n+1) supertile: every domino falls.

In detail

Why every level is assembled the same way. From the spectre paper: every tile lies in a unique endless hierarchy (Theorem 2.2); Theorems 3.1 and 4.1 turn a spectre tiling into a tiling by nine marked hexagons; Theorem 5.1 says those group into supertiles that are, combinatorially, reflected versions of the same nine hexagons. So every level is assembled by the same pattern (checked against the spectre paper’s figures, and against the computed hierarchy at every level from 1 to 8), and each side of a bigger supertile is a row of whole sides of its children.

The lengths. Each side length depends only on the side’s edge label, one of eight letters, and the eight letter lengths follow one fixed rule from level to level. Every one is a Fibonacci number, twice one, or one plus or minus two; every side is at least 5 edges long from level 1 on, and the Σ supertile’s crossover sits inside its side 5 at a distance that is twice a Fibonacci number.

Lengthening. A move replaces one edge of a shared side (or of the rim) by three edges, never at the ends of a side or next to the Σ crossover. Every walk of the old assembly becomes a walk of the new one with the same ends, so the hooks and the crossover stay where they were, and the new corners are hooks. Every assembly at the real side lengths of any level is reached from the level-1 assembly by such moves, undone one at a time from the top: some side is at least two edges longer than at level 1, and taking two edges off it near its start gives a smaller assembly of the same kind.

Computed, and checked by a proof assistant. The 18 level-1 assemblies have 8 children each (7 for Γ) and 732 ribbon ends (640); every ribbon was followed, from the tables alone, by a program and again inside the Lean proof assistant, whose kernel also checks the whole argument of this step in a combinatorial form. With every child’s crossover put one corner too far, all 18 fail: the check is not empty.

Second checks. The same assemblies were also computed directly at the side lengths of levels 2 to 4, and with every single two-edge lengthening of the level-1 ones (1,792 of them, each with the crossover either way): all have the signature. And a different route gives the same result: the small map of an assembly (its children, the runs along which they touch, each run even or odd, and the corners where something changes) depends only on evenness, so it repeats from level to level; the walk on its 18 versions gives the signature (two separate programs), and computed from the real supertiles it is the same at levels 2, 4, 6 and 8, and at 3, 5, 7 and 9.

Claim 1 · the Endless Strand Theorem
6

No loop, in any tiling: the Endless Strand Theorem

argued

A loop is finite, so it would have to fit somewhere. In most tilings it would fit inside one big supertile, and step 5 says no supertile has a loop inside. A few rare tilings have ‘fault lines’, where never-ending supertiles meet, and a loop could straddle one. But a loop that is short compared with the supertiles around it would have to turn back somewhere, and only a crossover turns a ribbon back. The crossovers of different supertiles are too far apart for a short loop to reach two of them, and one alone cannot close it. So in every spectre tiling, every ribbon runs on for ever in both directions.

An explanation

Suppose some ribbon did close up, into a loop of r pieces. Pick a level of supertiles whose sides are all longer than r tile edges. Follow the loop: inside a supertile it can only travel along a hook or the crossover (step 3), so each time it passes into the next supertile it has moved along the outline by just one corner of a tile. All the places where it crosses between supertiles are therefore strung together along the outlines, in a stretch shorter than any side: a little tree of edges, touching at most one point where supertiles meet. That is the Localisation Lemma.

Now follow the loop along that little tree. Crossing an edge moves it one step along the outline. A hook carries it round a corner and on along the next edge, so it never turns back at a hook. Only a crossover turns it back, in a hairpin bend: the loop crosses the crossover edge one way, and the crossover brings it straight back across the same edge. That is the Hairpin Lemma. A walk that returns to its start inside a tree must turn back somewhere, so the loop passes through a crossover.

The crossovers of different supertiles are at least a whole side apart (the Lonely Crossover Lemma), so a loop this short can only use the crossovers of one supertile. Take the point of the loop farthest from that crossover: the loop must turn back there, so it is at the crossover itself. But the crossover carries the loop on to the next corner, a step and a half from the crossover’s middle, which is farther. That contradiction shows there is no loop.

In detail

Most tilings. If the supertiles containing one tile grow to fill the whole plane, every tile lies in one of them, so any loop, being finite, lies inside a single supertile, which has none (step 5). This case is also checked by the Lean proof assistant. The argument above, for the rare tilings with a fault line, is in words only.

Why crossovers are far apart (the Lonely Crossover Lemma). A supertile’s crossover sits at its corner 0, except in the Σ kind, where it sits inside side 5, and from level 2 on at least the shortest side length from both ends. Two crossovers never share a corner: the two supertiles would meet along a side that is side 0 or side 5 of each, and the spectre’s matching rules never let those labels meet (α or γ on side 0, β or ζ on side 5). Two Σ crossovers never share a side either: that would glue a δ+ side to another δ+, which the rules also forbid. So any path from one crossover to another runs along at least one whole side.

As a cross-check, a computer examined every way two supertiles can sit side by side (264) and three can meet at a corner (489), for every level at once. It found no loop except in one arrangement of three Γ supertiles, where any loop would pass through two crossovers and so be far too long for the level it lives at.

The theorem is about tilings of the whole plane. A patch laid by hand that cannot be continued for ever is not covered. And it is for this design: the earlier design 0222221 has only its proof for the nest tiling.

Claim 2 · the Ariadne Theorem
7

Grow a nest, and everything joins: the Ariadne Theorem

computedcited: Thms 4.1, 5.1

Take a block, the block around it, the block around that, and so on. Each time, the pieces of the small block lie on fewer strands, until they lie on one. The blocks grow to cover the whole plane, so any two tiles end up on the same strand.

An explanation

The signature says how a supertile looks from outside. It does not yet say that the pieces inside belong to one ribbon. For that, look one level up at a time. When a block sits inside the next block of the nest, each of its ribbons, followed outwards, ends on one of just five hooks of the bigger block, at the corners where the bigger block’s outline passes from one child to the next; and which of the five depends only on which side of the small block the ribbon leaves from. (Ribbons on the one side of the small block that lies on the bigger block’s outline stay where they are, as hooks of the bigger block.)

This rule was computed once, at the level-1 sizes, and the argument of step 5 carries it to every level: two extra edges change nothing. Then follow it up. The five hooks lie well inside the sides of the bigger block, so one level further up the rule applies to them again, side by side. Three levels up (for an even-numbered block) or four (odd), every ribbon of the small block has arrived on the same hook.

In detail

The junction map. The rim of a block Nm+1 is a row of 22 sides of its children; a junction is a corner where one child’s side gives way to the next. Five of them, numbered 9, 12, 16, 17 and 21, lie well inside the sides of Nm+1, at least five edges from either end, so the hooks there are hooks of Nm+1. Every ribbon of the small block Nm ends on one of these five hooks, and which one depends only on the side of Nm it leaves from, and on whether m is even or odd. This was computed at the level-1 sizes, every ribbon followed, by a program and again inside the Lean proof assistant; the same two-edge lengthening as in step 5 carries it to every level, because the lengthening never cuts a side of Nm near its ends.

Every block. Read the rule side by side up the nest. For an even-numbered block its ribbons go to sides 2, 3 and 4 of the block one level up, then all to side 4 of the next, then all to one hook (junction 12) three levels up. For an odd-numbered block it takes one level more. The same steps give exactly the 2 strands two levels up, and for odd blocks three levels up, seen in the table. Lean checks this argument too, in a combinatorial form. As second checks, the first six blocks of the nest were followed ribbon by ribbon up to level 9, and a different method, which carries labels on the strands up the nest with the side lengths as exact formulas (583 comparisons for even-numbered blocks, 564 for odd ones, each following from ‘every side is at least 5 edges long’), gives the same result; two separate programs agree.

The Grandparent Lemma: the nest covers the plane. Each block shares no corner with the rim of its grandparent: in the fixed assembly pattern, the only side of the small block on its parent’s rim lies inside a side of the parent that faces a sibling one level up. So every tile next to block Nb is inside Nb+2, and the blocks swallow every tile eventually. (Also measured directly for the first five blocks.)

The finish. Take any two tiles. Both lie inside some block Nb of the nest. Every ribbon piece in them is part of a piece of Nb, because nothing closes up inside, and all pieces of Nb lie on one ribbon of Nb+4. So the two tiles are on the same ribbon. And there is no ring: a ring is finite, so it lies inside some block, and no supertile has one (step 5).

Scope
8

Exactly what is covered

The Endless Strand Theorem (no loops) holds in every spectre tiling, however the tiles cover the plane, including the rare tilings with ‘fault lines’. The Ariadne Theorem (one strand) is proved only for the nest tiling, drawn on this site. Other tilings may have more than one strand: computer checks suggest that some have three.

An explanation

A spectre tiling is determined by its chain of ancestors. The tiling here grows by always taking child number 1 (the nest). Other tilings grow from a different chain of ancestors. The Endless Strand Theorem holds for all of them (step 6). The Ariadne Theorem depends on the chain, and it appears not to hold for every chain: along one chain, with Θ and Γ supertiles alternating (child 2, then child 7), the computer finds three strands that do not join, followed exactly to level 8 and by the label method of step 7 to level 20. That is evidence, not a proof, and the site claims nothing about it. A few special tilings contain two or more infinite supertiles meeting along lines or at a point (fault tilings): they have no loops either (step 6), and nothing is claimed about their number of strands.

In detail

The design is the same on every tile in the strict sense: the nest tiling uses only one handedness of the spectre, so it is one drawing, turned and slid, never flipped. Over and under is a separate question: any drawing of this kind can be given a weave that alternates along every ribbon. The single-strand result is about which pieces join.

Glossary
Port

An end of a ribbon piece, where it meets a tile edge. Two ribbons cross each edge at one point, 0.15 of the edge off its middle, so each edge has two ports on each side.

Supertile

A cluster of tiles (9, 71, 559, …) that behaves like one big tile. There are nine kinds, named by Greek letters, and every level is built from the one below by the same rule.

Level

Level 0 is a single tile, level 1 a supertile of tiles, level 2 a supertile of level-1 supertiles, and so on.

Rim

The outline of a supertile. Only the ports on the rim matter from outside.

Hook

At a corner of the rim, a ribbon that comes in through the port just before the corner and goes straight back out through the port just after it.

Crossover

At one special corner (the Γ corner) the two ribbons do not hook back; they cross and leave by the far side.

Signature

What a supertile looks like from outside: a hook at every corner but two, one crossover, and no ring inside.

Run

A stretch of edges along which two neighbouring supertiles touch.

Even, odd

Whether a run (or a side) has an even or odd number of tile edges. The zigzag along a run only cares about this.

Mark

A point on a child’s rim where something happens: its neighbour changes, or its crossover sits.

Small map

The children, their runs (even or odd) and their marks: all you need to work out how ribbons join inside a supertile. Used in a second check of step 5.

Nest

A chain of blocks, each one child of the next, growing to fill the whole plane. The tiling on this site is grown this way.
