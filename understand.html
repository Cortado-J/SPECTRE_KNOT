What is the Spectre Knot?

It is a Celtic knot drawn on a tile. Every tile carries the same small drawing of ribbon, and when the tiles are laid together the ribbons meet across the edges and carry on, weaving over and under.

The tile is the spectre, a shape that covers the whole plane but only in patterns that never repeat. The surprise is twofold, and each half is a theorem. The Endless Strand Theorem: however the tiles cover the plane, no strand ever closes into a loop. The Ariadne Theorem: in the nest tiling drawn on this site, grown outwards from a single tile, all the pieces join into one endless strand, with no second strand. (Ariadne gave Theseus the one thread that led him through the whole labyrinth.)

What is the spectre, and what does “never repeats” mean?

Squares and hexagons tile the plane periodically: slide the pattern in the right direction and it lands exactly on itself. For a long time nobody knew whether a single shape could force a tiling that never does this. It was called the einstein problem (‘one stone’).

In 2023 David Smith, Joseph Samuel Myers, Craig S. Kaplan and Chaim Goodman‑Strauss found one: first the hat, which needs some copies flipped over, then weeks later the spectre, which needs none. Copies of the spectre cover the whole plane, but every such covering is non-repeating.

Their paper: A chiral aperiodic monotile.

Is it really a spectre? The edges are straight.

The tile drawn here is the straight-edged form of the spectre, which the paper calls Tile(1,1). Laid without flipping, its copies tile exactly as spectres do, and only in patterns that never repeat.

Give each edge the paper’s S-curve and you have the strict spectre, which cannot be flipped at all. The knot works the same way on both, because it only depends on how the tiles fit together, not on the exact shape of the edges.

The straight edges make the tile easy to cut out and fold. And because it is printed on one side, a flipped tile would show its blank back, so every tile goes down the same way up. That is what keeps out the mirror-image copies, and it is why straight edges are enough.

What exactly is drawn on each tile?

Fourteen pieces of ribbon. Each runs from one edge of the tile to another. Each of the 14 edges has one port, a point a little way off the middle of the edge, and two pieces cross there, one over and one under.

Inside the tile there is just one crossing. That is about as simple as a design can be.

The colours here only tell the 14 pieces apart. On a single tile they are 14 separate pieces.

How do the ribbons meet when tiles touch?

The port on each edge sits 0.15 of the edge’s length off the midpoint, to one side or the other depending on the edge. Around the tile the sides alternate, edge by edge. The offset is there only for looks: it makes the curves more pleasing. With every port at the midpoint, the ribbons would join up in exactly the same way.

When two spectres meet along an edge, one edge is always an ‘even’ edge and the other an ‘odd’ one, so their ports land on exactly the same point. The ribbon leaving one tile carries straight on into the next.

Why just one crossing, and does it matter where it is?

The plainest Celtic recipe has no crossing inside the tile at all: every ribbon just turns back round a corner. On the spectre that fails at once: in every one of the first supertiles, of 8 or 9 tiles, a strand closes into a loop.

So change one thing: two ribbons that would have turned round two neighbouring corners cross each other instead. That one crossing is enough. It gives the design a pattern that copies itself: seen from outside, every supertile, however big, looks like the tile again, with a hook round every corner but two and a crossing at those two. That repetition is what the proof runs on (Prove, step 3). The AI search turned up 34 candidate designs; Justin Roughley chose this one and drew its ribbons by hand.

Where the crossing goes matters a great deal. There are 14 places it could go, one beside each edge. With it at 8 of them, closed loops appear in supertiles of at most 559 tiles. At the other 6, this one among them, the computer found no loop in any supertile up to 272,791 tiles. Only the design drawn here has been proved.

What does not matter is the exact drawing: where precisely inside the tile the two ribbons cross, and how the curves bend. All that counts is which port each piece joins to which. Any drawing with the same joins gives exactly the same strands. Over and under don’t change the strands either; they only make the weave alternate.

So how many strands does the whole tiling make?

One, in the nest tiling drawn here: that is the Ariadne Theorem. (The spectre tiles the plane in infinitely many ways, and the answer may differ: computer checks suggest that some other spectre tilings have three strands. That is evidence, not a proof. In every one of them, though, each strand is endless, by the Endless Strand Theorem: none closes up.) Look at a small patch and you see many separate pieces, because each piece soon runs out of the patch. Look at a bigger patch and the pieces join up.

Follow the 14 pieces of one tile: in a block of 9 tiles they lie on 5 strands, in 71 tiles on 2, and from 559 tiles on they are one strand. The same happens to every block of the nest: its pieces lie on one strand at most four levels up (computed directly for blocks of up to 34,649 tiles, inside blocks of up to 133 million). Why it goes on for ever is on the Prove page.

Why does it keep joining, instead of stopping at a few strands?

The spectre tiles group into supertiles of 9 tiles, these into bigger supertiles of 71, then 559, and so on for ever, every level built by the same rule. Seen from outside, every supertile turns out to look the same: the ribbon makes a hook round each corner of its outline, except at the two ends of one edge, where two ribbons cross.

Because every supertile looks the same from outside, gluing supertiles together depends only on whether each shared run of edges is even or odd: a run two edges longer behaves exactly like the shorter one. The sides of the supertiles at any level are those at the smallest level made longer by even amounts, so one finite check at the smallest level speaks for all levels. The same idea shows that the gluing keeps merging strands until a given patch lies on one. That every level is glued by the same rule comes from the spectre paper’s theorems.

Why can’t a repeating tiling do this?

In a repeating pattern, there are two different slides, a and b, that each bring the whole pattern back onto itself. A slide moves every strand onto a strand.

If there were only one strand, both slides would have to move that one strand onto itself: slide it along its own length, in two independent directions at once. A single line cannot do that. Combining the two slides cleverly gives a shift that moves the strand along itself by a tiny amount but moves the plane by a full step, which is impossible.

So it takes a tiling that never repeats. The spectre’s is one.

Could a closed loop appear somewhere far away?

No, in any spectre tiling at all: that is the Endless Strand Theorem. The argument on the Prove page shows that no supertile, of any kind or size, has a loop inside, using exact checks of the smallest cases and the spectre paper’s theorems; it marks what is computed, what is quoted and what is argued. As a cross-check, every supertile of every kind was assembled by computer up to level 9 (about 133 million tiles) without a single closed loop.

That even covers the rare tilings with ‘fault lines’, where two or three infinite supertiles meet: a short loop would have to turn back somewhere, only a crossover turns a ribbon back, and the crossovers of different supertiles are too far apart for one loop to use two of them. But no closed loop is not the same as one strand: one strand, the Ariadne Theorem, is proved only for the nest tiling drawn here.

Who found what?

The spectre was discovered in 2023 by David Smith, Joseph Samuel Myers, Craig S. Kaplan and Chaim Goodman‑Strauss. Justin Roughley came up with the original idea of looking for a mono‑stranded aperiodic knot, and then used AI to search for it (2026).
